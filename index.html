What the research says, and where each page applies it

1. The hero gets about five seconds

The headline, sub line, CTA, and visual must all say one thing. A headline that fits three different products is too vague. All five pages lead with "what Scrap does", not with a slogan.

2. Concrete CTAs beat "Get started"

Evil Martians changed "Build with us" to "Hire Martians" and clicks went from 1.3% to 2.0%. The pages use "Download Scrap — free" plus microcopy that removes doubt (platforms, no account).

3. Developers want a second path

High-converting dev-tool pages give at least two of these: live demo, docs, source. Each hero has a secondary "Watch the 90 s demo" or "Read the docs" link.

4. Specific proof, near the CTA

One line of proof under the CTA is a common lift. A 2026 benchmark found that generic "trusted by thousands" proof did no better than no proof. The pages show counted numbers and named people.

5. Show the product early

Put a 10–15 line code sample or a real editor view above the second scroll. Each page has an editor mock, a code block, or both.

6. A clean first screen

Developers in user interviews got lost when newsletter forms, social buttons, and carousels filled the first screen. Navigation stays at 4–5 links plus one CTA.

7. 3D holds attention, if it is fast

In one usability study (n = 1 site, self-report), 69% of users said 3D held their attention, and 54% said they scrolled the full page. Unoptimized WebGL raises bounce, mostly on mid-range phones.

8. How the pages pay for the 3D

  • The HTML text is the LCP element. Three.js loads after it.
  • DPR capped at 2. The render loop stops when the canvas is off screen.
  • prefers-reduced-motion renders still frames.
